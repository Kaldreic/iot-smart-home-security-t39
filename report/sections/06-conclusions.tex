\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Reproducing a wireless IoT bug is limited by the oracle: channel noise and hidden
device state make its verdict inconsistent, and once that happens the exact
minimisation algorithms that assume a stable verdict~\cite{ddmin2002} no longer
converge on the right recipe. RDD does not remove the noise; it bounds the rate
at which the verdict is wrong, with a truncated sequential test, a two-tier crash
identity and an independent re-confirmation, so that delta debugging can run
again. On six real Zephyr controller bugs under a simulated channel the prototype
recovered 0.848 of reproductions with no false credit against 0.759 and 0.137
for a re-implementation of \textsc{AirBugCatcher}'s strategy, at five times the
device reads, and it kept its lead across a 75,000-campaign synthetic sweep.

Those results hold under the simulator's two noise assumptions, phantom crashes
that carry the target's signature and modelled report variation, and they
reverse without them: with both switched off, enumeration with one confirming
re-run recovers more at a fifth of the cost. The false-credit result is a bound,
$\alpha$ per decision under stated preconditions, and zero is what was measured.
Two limitations are intrinsic to the method: the indifference zone of the
sequential test, which leaves the deepest noise unrecoverable, and the identity
layer's dependence on the deterministic matcher, since the judge accepts every
pair it is shown. The proposed experiments therefore start by measuring on a real
link the two rates the simulator assumes, and by presenting the identity oracle
with crashes of other bugs; only then would a field-deployable wireless
reproduction oracle be a claim rather than a direction.
